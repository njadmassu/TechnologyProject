The AI Boom and the GPU Market

How artificial intelligence is changing technology and gaming

How GPUs Became Important to AI

When Sam Altman said that it was “quite terrible” for individuals to have RAM sitting in personal computers rather than for use in data centers, hobbyists and media professionals alike were quick to respond with a measured outrage. The proliferation of AI in daily life has only exacerbated the divides between those who desire the cutting edge of technology and those who see encroachment. People’s perceptions of tech in our lives, and those who most engage with it most closely have also shifted. Tech giant Nvidia prided itself on marketing the first ever GPU in 1999, a term now as ubiquitous to tech conscious people as a motherboard or cpu (Central Processing Unit). The structure of a GPU involves many parts of hardware and firmware working together; it is a large group of cores all working in tandem to produce many layered calculations at the same time, the VRAM or video random access memory is responsible for storing actively processed data whether that be graphic models, mathematical figures, or components responsible for training AI. The predecessor to modern AI, Machine Learning was originally structured around large collections of CPUs, simply because prior to the advent of the 2010s, the parallel processing capabilities that are now seen in GPUs did not exist. Nvidia’s own role in this largely came from recognizing the capabilities of its GPUs in its supercomputers, and tactful partnerships with other tech giants such as Google and its development of AI.

Gaming and the Changing Technology Market

Cultural power, and social capital are always shifting, one may see it as an ebb and flow, or as a continuous distancing and redefining of people and who they are. AI companies have marketed themselves to the masses, and the image of who uses the hardware to employ it have changed. One of the groups that have been most responsive towards comments like those made by Altman have been PC gamers, who have endured their labeling as closed-off outcasts to come into the light as the most average consumers of new technological developments. Perhaps some of the outrage that they feel stems from the shifting values of companies that have historically been in their corner. Gamers have enjoyed a level of contained hobbyism due to the strict requirements of practical knowledge to get into the scene; The know-how to acquire or assemble an adequate PC and to then acclimate to the culture and mechanics of video games in myriad releases. Gamers as a whole had no small part to play here either, aside from still apparent cultural expressions such as “#gamergate” which began as fierce criticism of games journalism before feeding into the perceived encroachment of women and minorities into the scene; gamers wanted to keep their community as a ‘boys club’ and not just any club but a club which would cater to the perceived archetypical gamer: the straight white male gamer. This sort of elitism and assumption seem natural when seeing how such perceptions of the community have remained consistent. Games journalist and critic Ben “Yahtzee” Croshaw coined the term “Glorious Gaming PC Master Race” in 2008 when reviewing the momentous release of the first Witcher game. In line with his personal brand of offensive humor, he saw equating the PC community to the same type of rhetoric carried out by racial supremacist groups; the similarities seen in self-labeling and truly believing that their in-group was the most fit, the most committed and able to represent and dictate discourse and action on their scene. So one explanation here is the fact that gamers are no longer the most catered to by their previously perceived partners in tech corporations, Nvidia has quietly pulled its current most powerful models of GPU, the 5080 and 5090 off of online store fronts as rumors gather that the high demand from AI data centers. These GPUs alone have doubled from their release market price of $1,000 to $2,000 or even more. The scarcity of resources for production leave it stretched too thin to support the gaming market..Why would they anyway ? The gaming industry on the Triple A field continues to consolidate and cannibalize itself with risky investments, longer development times, and volatile responses to sales numbers. There is simply so much more possible profit in the AI sector.

The Financial Risks of Artificial Intelligence

The priorities have shifted, and so too have the responses to these decisions. The demand for GPU chip production and integration of AI is certainly there, but even more so it is almost entirely speculative. As almost all people have seen, the AI boom has showcased the recurring truth that current hypercapitalist economies are always going to be interested in the idea of limitless applications and growth. LLMs and similar AI tools are being integrated into apps, homes, military, infrastructure, gaming, and numerous other fields; to investors it may appear as a real source of bottomless potential since products can feature some type of developed AI technology due to its flexibility. Tech Ceo’s also market themselves aggressively, stating that gpu chips themselves are able to support multi million investment plans yet investors aren’t as responsive as they’d hope either. Not only are the lifetimes of GPUs deprecated through constant use by data centers, but the building of so many centers being as contentious as it is places an insecure burden onto the development of these technologies. These aren't the only concerns people have either; a growing observation and criticism about the current AI economy is the issue of circular financing. That is that AI developers such as OpenAI, and producers of hardware like Nvidia are most deeply economically linked with each other, they are the ones giving each other the most service which on the surface boosts all of their stocks and positions as economic powerhouses; at the same time it creates adverse conditions wherein failure for one corporation would spell a disastrous domino effect for all of their partners. The balancing act that these interlinked corporations must play in achieving more investment, and pushing the technology with further results may only last so long when key infrastructure like Nvidia’s GPUs becomes more expensive to produce as demand only rises.

Lessons from the Cryptocurrency Boom

Earlier we saw how cultures may revolve around markets and importantly the perceptions around those markets. The alignment of current hardware to use for AI is being recognized as one entry in a history of risky ventures for the sake of discovering and claiming new frontiers. GPUs themselves have a very recent entry in being appropriated for some specific uses. That of as workhorses in mining crypto currencies. These currencies have no central distributors and rely on complex code to encrypt and encrypt them for the purposes of transactions. The speed and layers of processing that they provide proved advantageous for extracting any sort of currency, with the advent of crypto “farms” or buildings filled with shelves of GPUS all working in tandem in order to generate these assets. With such potential value being seen in GPUs, their prices once again went up, generally increasing by 50% or so. Sounds like a data center in almost every metric. Markets reacted similarly to the crypto boom of the late 2010s and early 2020s in the fact that prices went up and individual consumers were almost always bought out by groups who collected GPUs for their worth as tools to expand on new capital. Previous corporations that were entirely focused on crypto mining have also pivoted to AI Integration, the combination of these ventures is also rather unclear; but for these firms the markets are still entirely open and those who have the GPUs to pursue them will do so no matter what. Seeing these opportunities to break out success stories, new possibilities and new failures, we look to the past and reform memories to project a future. GPU prices for one did stabilize back to their original levels after the explosion of crypto subsided in prominence and investments dried up. Another point of comparison are financial bubbles, whether that be the .com boom or the housing bubble and resulting crisis. Those who have lived through these scenarios are reminded of the dangers posed by investments being made on top of one another, and how they look to the future while on an unsteady foundation. AI debt is being attached to financial products that millions of people rely on, and the possibility of failure always looms, especially when marking the fact that many AI companies are or project to lose more revenue than ever before.

The Future of the GPU Market

People come to see that they are on different sides of the coin when it comes to elite investors and CEOs, those who pursue wealth no matter what the fall back could be to others. The previous cultural interlude marked shifting priorities and responses, and the same wave seems to be crashing again. AI data center moratoriums are being voted overwhelmingly in favor of as people grow opposed to their pollution in noise, water, and sustainability. Film and Game studios themselves are increasingly pressured to distance themselves from or disavow AI involvement in their workflow while also capturing stories on the banality of evil that is apparent in those who put profits over people. The Social Reckoning (2026) for instance continues the story of how tech moguls think so callously towards their user base as they did in their own personal relationships as seen in The Social Network (2010). The smash hit documentary You Can See Everything (2026) also features highly manipulative former CEOs and creators that are looking to tech as new frontiers to regain the wealth and respect that they’ve lost. The shortage and valuation of technological products of GPUs is but one chapter in the development of our current economy. Their value has risen sharply and settled back down all the same, with the possibility of AI growing more uncertain and public consciousness turning, perhaps it is time for yet another fall.
